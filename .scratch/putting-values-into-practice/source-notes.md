## Distinguishing values, traits, behaviour and impact

Input C distinguishes four questions:

| Concept | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Values | What matters to me? |
| Traits | What am I generally predisposed to do? |
| Behaviour | What do I actually do? |
| Impact | How do others experience what I do? |

For this feature, a hypothetical response records what someone thinks they would do. A real example records their account of what they did. Neither directly observes their behaviour or establishes another person's experience.

The useful connection is: what matters to me → what I choose and why → when that choice might become counterproductive → how it might land → what I might try differently. This is a design synthesis from the conversations, not a formal model jointly defined by the authors.

## Dotlich: when useful strengths become counterproductive

Inputs A and C describe David Dotlich and Peter Cairo's *Why CEOs Fail* as identifying 11 leadership derailers, with problematic tendencies becoming especially relevant under pressure. For the agreed feature, the useful design question is: **When could an approach that helps me begin to hinder me or others?**

Illustrative strength-to-overuse examples from input A:

| Useful strength | Possible counterproductive expression |
| --- | --- |
| Confidence | Arrogance |
| Careful judgement | Excessive caution |
| Attention to detail | Perfectionism |
| Collaboration | Eagerness to please |

These pairs are interpretations in the ChatGPT conversation, not an original spectrum or diagram from the book. They can inspire optional examples without assigning a derailer label to a person.

Inputs A and C also suggest pressure as a reflection lens: an approach that helps in ordinary circumstances might become more rigid or costly when time, responsibility or uncertainty increases. This can inform a later reflection question; the agreed flow does not require an additional pressure question after every situation.

### Why Leadership Span is excluded

Input A distinguishes Dotlich's work from the four contrasting pairs attributed to Russell Reynolds Associates and Hogan Assessments' Leadership Span: disruptive/pragmatic, risk-taking/reluctant, heroic/vulnerable and galvanising/connecting. It suggests this may explain the owner's original recollection of paired opposites.

The owner explicitly excluded Leadership Span from the design. Preserve this distinction so a future content author does not mistakenly attribute those pairs to Dotlich or reintroduce them as feature requirements.

## Geisler: helpful intentions and an unintended impression

Input B describes Jill Geisler's *Work Happy: What Great Bosses Know* and the evil-twin concept as a way to consider how a positive intention can be experienced differently. Input C adds a useful nuance: the difference need not result from excessive behaviour; context, expectations or communication may also matter.

The conversations' examples provide a starting point for optional reflection prompts:

| Intended strength | A possible unintended impression |
| --- | --- |
| High standards | Impossible to please |
| Attention to detail | Micromanaging |
| Decisiveness | Controlling or dismissive |
| Supporting others | Interfering or rescuing |
| Collaboration | Indecision |
| Empowering others | Absence or disengagement |
| Confidence | Arrogance |
| Calm under pressure | Lack of concern or detachment |
| Being approachable | Avoidance of difficult conversations |
| Challenging assumptions | Constant negativity |

Input B attributes the first two examples to Geisler's published discussions and describes the remainder as illustrative applications. Treat every row as a possible interpretation to explore. The table does not establish that anyone actually holds the negative impression.

Input B also describes a manager who intends to help by working alongside a team, while the team experiences the involvement as micromanagement. This attributed example needs source checking before public use.

The practical reflection is to consider helpful intent, clarify it where appropriate, and remain open to actual feedback. In the feature, received feedback must remain distinct from an imagined possible perspective.

## Same action, different motivations

Input C gives a useful counterexample to automatic interpretation: two people offer to finish someone else's work. One hopes to support the team; the other feels uncomfortable trusting someone else to deliver. The visible action is similar, but the motivation may differ.

Ask what mattered to the person and what they hoped to achieve. An associated value can inform that explanation, but does not determine it. A prepared action choice must not silently become an inferred intention or a diagnosis.

## Worked situation and reflection prompts

Input C offers the following shared-responsibility example:

> A shared task has stalled because the next step has no clear owner. You have capacity to help, although you are not responsible for leading the task.

Prepared starting responses in the note are to offer one clear next step, ask the group to agree ownership, or finish the task yourself if quicker. The agreed design also permits another response in the person's own words.

Useful prompt ideas, adapted from inputs B and C:

- What would you most likely do?
- What mattered to you, and what were you hoping to achieve?
- Does a value connect to that response? Does the response feel aligned, in tension or uncertain?
- Can you recall a time when you actually responded this way?
- When might this approach help? When could it begin to work against you or others?
- How might someone else experience it? Have you received any feedback about that?
- What small action would you like to try?

The [intended journey](spec.md#intended-journey) governs pacing. Explore response and motivation within each situation, with optional value connections and examples; reserve the broader overuse and perspective reflection for after several situations. The original note's five-step example is not a required five-question sequence for every situation.

## Illustrative summary: taking responsibility

Input C offers a useful result structure. The adaptation below is illustrative content, not a conclusion about an actual user. A real summary must follow the [summary and evidence rules](spec.md#summary-and-evidence-rules), including support from at least two distinct situations within the current reflection.

**A theme worth exploring: taking responsibility**

Your responses suggest you may be inclined to step in when responsibilities are unclear, particularly when progress matters to you.

**Where this could help:** You may bring momentum and reliability when others are uncertain about the next step.

**When it might work against you:** You might take on work that would be better owned by someone else, leaving less capacity for your own priorities.

**How it might land:** Someone could appreciate your initiative; another person might feel that you are taking over or have less opportunity to contribute. These are possibilities to consider.

**An action you could choose:** Before taking over, ask what help would be useful and agree who owns the next step. The person can choose a different action or leave this open.

This structure can support content authoring: a tentative theme, constructive use, possible cost, possible outside perspective and a user-chosen next step. It does not settle how the app recognises themes in prepared or written responses.
